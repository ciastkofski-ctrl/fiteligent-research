Tirzepatide for Recurrent Weight Gain after Bariatric Procedures: Real-World Evidence of Efficacy and Safety

⭐⭐⭐ · Vinciguerra et al. · Obes Surg (2026-06-05)

  • Observational cohort study; 34 patients (26 female, 8 male) with documented recurrent weight gain after bariatric surgery (n=32) or endoscopic bariatric therapy (n=2).
  • Intervention: once-weekly subcutaneous tirzepatide, dosing range 2.5–10 mg/week, over 24 weeks; anthropometric and adverse-event data collected at baseline and week 24.
  • Primary outcome: mean percentage total body weight loss (%TBWL) = 18.1 ± 5.6% (p < 0.0001); waist circumference also significantly reduced (p < 0.0001).
  • Most patients shifted to lower BMI categories; the majority reached overweight or normal-weight status by week 24.
  • All adverse events were gastrointestinal (constipation, diarrhea, nausea), described as generally mild; no treatment discontinuations due to adverse events reported.
  • Limitations: no control group, observational design, small and predominantly female sample, single-center real-world data.

FGF19 promotes cartilage destruction in the progress of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis

⭐⭐⭐ · Chen et al. · Cell Tissue Res (2026-06-05)

  • Mouse model study: TMJ osteoarthritis (TMJOA) induced by unilateral anterior crossbite (UAC); FGF19 overexpressed via direct adeno-associated virus injection carrying the Fgf19 gene.
  • FGF19 overexpression accelerated cartilage phenotype transformation: reduced COL2A1 and aggrecan; increased COL10A1, MMP13, ADAMTS5, COL1A1, and COL1A2 expression.
  • FGF19 induced calcification of cartilage hypertrophic layer via up-regulation of DMP1 through transcription factor osterix (OSX).
  • FGF19 was found substantially up-regulated in the joint cavity of OA patients in prior literature; this study provides mechanistic causation in an animal model.
  • No human intervention data; no direct strength or hypertrophy relevance.

RNA splicing and cardiovascular disease: a guide for cardiologists

⭐⭐⭐ · Hunt et al. · Eur Heart J (2026-06-05)

  • Narrative review targeting cardiologists; covers alternative splicing (AS) as a mechanism generating protein isoform diversity in cardiac biology.
  • Key mechanistic claims: cardiac-enriched RNA-binding proteins establish heart-specific splicing programs; pathological hypertrophy engages distinct splicing signatures compared with physiological hypertrophy.
  • Diagnostic/prognostic angle: bulk, long-read, and single-cell transcriptomics can resolve cell-type–specific isoforms; circulating RNA biomarkers (including splice ratios and circularRNAs) may indicate myocardial remodeling and arrhythmic risk.
  • Therapeutic strategies reviewed: antisense oligonucleotides, small molecules, CRISPR, base editing, and prime editing to modulate splicing in CVD.
  • Neuromuscular disease precedent (e.g., spinal muscular atrophy splice-switching drugs) cited as clinical feasibility proof-of-concept for cardiac translation.

Probiotics, synbiotics and berberine in Type 2 diabetes mellitus: A systematic review, meta-analysis, and molecular dynamics simulation study

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Shadin et al. · PLoS One (2026)

  • Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials; >30 trials, >2,000 participants; PROSPERO registration CRD420251116387.
  • Pooled analysis (random-effects models) for fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and HbA1c across interventions (probiotics, synbiotics, berberine) in type 2 diabetes: FPG reduction −0.71 mmol/L; HbA1c reduction −0.19%; both statistically significant.
  • Probiotic-specific subgroup vs. placebo: FPG approximately −0.80 mmol/L; HbA1c approximately −0.21%.
  • Substantial between-study variability (heterogeneity) reported across all outcomes; authors describe effect sizes as "clinically modest."
  • Exploratory molecular dynamics/docking analysis: berberine showed weaker binding affinity to α-glucosidase than the reference inhibitor acarbose.
  • Conclusion: probiotics, synbiotics, and berberine are positioned as adjunctive rather than primary therapeutic options; authors call for larger, longer, standardized trials.

  • Training protocol: eight resistance exercises at 60% 1RM with active rest periods, plus progressive aerobic bouts at 50%–70% HRmax.
  • Primary and secondary outcomes assessed pre- and post-intervention: plasma Maresin-1 (MaR1), IL-6, TNF-α, fasting blood glucose (FBS), insulin, and HOMA-IR.
  • Significant group × time interactions reported for Maresin-1 (specific p-values and effect sizes not fully available in the truncated abstract); the TF group produced the greatest improvements across all biomarkers.
  • Reported direction of change in TF group: ↑ Maresin-1, ↓ IL-6, ↓ TNF-α, ↓ FBS, ↓ insulin, ↓ HOMA-IR.
  • Limitations: all-male sample, single BMI category (obese), short follow-up, small n per arm (~11 each); precise between-group effect sizes and CIs not recoverable from the available abstract text.

Sleep & Recovery

Acute Sleep Deprivation Shifts Substrate Utilization Toward Greater Fat Oxidation During Incremental Exercise in Recreationally Trained Adults

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Del Val-Manzano et al. · Scand J Med Sci Sports (2026-06-01)

  • Randomized, counterbalanced crossover study; 32 recreationally trained young adults (16 female, 16 male); three within-subject sleep conditions: normal sleep (NS, 8 h), early/partial sleep deprivation (ESD, 4 h), and total sleep deprivation (SD, 0 h).
  • Sleep duration verified by wrist actigraphy plus direct researcher monitoring; incremental cycling test performed the morning following each condition to determine maximal fat oxidation (MFO) and maximal carbohydrate oxidation (MCHO), both in absolute and relative (per kg fat-free mass) terms.
  • Key finding: both ESD and SD conditions shifted substrate utilization toward greater fat oxidation and reduced carbohydrate oxidation during the incremental cycling test compared with NS; specific MFO values and p-values not fully recoverable from truncated abstract.
  • The direction of the finding is counterintuitive relative to common assumptions about sleep deprivation impairing metabolic efficiency.
  • Limitations: acute single-night exposure, young recreationally trained adults only, substrate utilization measured via indirect calorimetry during a standardized test (not ad libitum exercise); no data on longer-term or chronic sleep restriction.

Effects on secondary outcomes following a three-month personalized app-based lifestyle intervention among working adults: a three-armed randomized controlled trial

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Eke et al. · Sci Rep (2026-05-31)

  • 3-month three-armed RCT; n=209 office workers and bus drivers; randomized 1:1:1 to app only, app + monthly coaching, or control; mean participant age 48.2 years; 42% female.
  • Exercise: both intervention groups increased minutes of exercise vs. control — app group β = 15.7 min/week (95% CI: 3.5, 27.9); app+coach group β = 13.8 min/week (95% CI: 1.4, 26.1).
  • Diet: only app+coach group increased vegetable intake vs. control — β = 51.8 g/day (95% CI: 2.5, 101.1); fruit and vegetable combined β = 87.7 g/day (95% CI: 8.9, 166.6).
  • Sleep: only app+coach group increased time in bed β = 0.43 h/night (95% CI: 0.05, 0.81) and sleep duration β = 0.40 h/night (95% CI: 0.01, 0.78) vs. control.
  • No other outcomes (alcohol, stress, smoking) differed significantly between groups.
  • Limitations: 3-month follow-up only; self-reported diet and sleep for many outcomes; modest effect sizes on the lower confidence-interval boundary approaching zero; coaching contribution and its mechanism not fully isolated.

Circadian rhythms in major depressive disorder: mechanistic insights and therapeutic frontiers

⭐⭐⭐ · Saeed et al. · Ann Med (2026-12-01)

  • Narrative review synthesizing evidence on circadian system disruption as a pathophysiological mechanism in major depressive disorder (MDD); no primary trial data.
  • Epidemiological scope cited: MDD affects over 264 million people globally; framed as a leading cause of disability.
  • Genetic evidence reviewed: variants in core clock genes CLOCK, TIMELESS, and CRY1 identified as correlating with both circadian disruption and MDD susceptibility.
  • Mechanistic axes reviewed: dysregulated hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function and abnormal melatonin signaling are proposed as potentially causal rather than merely symptomatic in MDD pathogenesis.
  • Chronotherapeutic approaches summarized: light therapy, sleep interventions (e.g., sleep deprivation therapy, phase advance), and targeted pharmacology (e.g., agomelatine); personalized circadian-based treatment guided by genetic and molecular markers proposed as a future direction.

Skin & Hair Health

Evaluation of a Novel Bimodal-Approach Radiofrequency Device for Lower Facial Tightening in Southeast Asian Patients: An Open-Label Prospective Study

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Chottawornsak et al. · J Cosmet Dermatol (2026-06-01)

  • Open-label prospective cohort study; 26 adults enrolled (age 30–60 years) with mild-to-moderate lower facial laxity; 23 completed follow-up.
  • Device: DENSITY Noir (Jeisys Med Inc., Seoul), a bimodal RF system delivering monopolar and bipolar energy sequentially within a single shot; single treatment session administered.
  • Primary efficacy: 47.83% of completers achieved marked improvement (51%–75% on 6-point global improvement scale) at 6 months; facial laxity ratings significantly improved at 1, 3, and 6 months vs. baseline (p < 0.001 for each).
  • 3D Antera imaging outcomes: nasolabial fold cross-sectional area reduced from 41.94 (baseline) to 35.72 at 6 months (p = 0.02); wrinkle width reduced from 1.98 mm to 1.85 mm at 6 months (p = 0.01).
  • Participant satisfaction: ~78% reported high satisfaction at 6 months; adverse events — transient erythema (96.15%), edema (61.53%), mild procedural pain — all minor, transient, and self-limiting.
  • Limitations: no control arm, open-label design, predominantly Southeast Asian population (may limit generalizability), single treatment session only, small n.

Anti-asthma drug montelukast induces autistic behaviors via disrupting neuronal retinoic acid signaling

⭐⭐⭐ · Hao et al. · Signal Transduct Target Ther (2026-06-02)

  • Multi-modal study: in vitro (primary rat prefrontal cortex neurons), in vivo (wild-type rats, prenatal and early postnatal exposure), brain organoid (human embryonic stem cell-derived), molecular docking, and multi-center clinical cohort components.
  • Core mechanistic finding: montelukast (MTK), a cysteinyl leukotriene receptor antagonist, disrupts neuronal retinoic acid (RA) signaling by physically interacting with RA receptors (RARs), particularly RARα, as confirmed by molecular docking and biochemical validation.
  • In vivo: prenatal or early postnatal MTK exposure induced autistic-like behaviors in wild-type rats; behaviors were significantly alleviated by supplementation with all-trans retinoic acid (atRA).
  • Organoid data: MTK altered neuronal RA signaling and forebrain patterning in human brain organoids.
  • Clinical component: multi-center survey in a large Chinese ASD cohort suggested MTK administration during early childhood may increase ASD risk; specific odds ratios, confidence intervals, and cohort size not recoverable from available abstract.
