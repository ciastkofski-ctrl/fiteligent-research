Obesity & Metabolic Health

Machine learning identifies microbiome and clinical predictors of sustained weight loss following prolonged fasting

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Kaufhold et al. · Genome Med (2026) · RCT

  • Randomized, waitlist-controlled trial; 38 healthy adults completed a 5-day fasting intervention with 12-week follow-up (LEANER study; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04452916, registered June 29, 2020).
  • Primary outcomes: BMI, body composition, gut microbiome composition (16S/metagenomics), and plasma and fecal metabolites at acute post-fast and 12-week timepoints.
  • Fasting reduced BMI acutely, predominantly driven by fat mass loss; improvements partially persisted at 12 weeks.
  • Fasting induced marked shifts in gut microbiome composition and in plasma and fecal metabolite profiles; post-fasting microbial diversity changes were associated with baseline diversity.
  • A machine-learning model integrating baseline microbiome and clinical variables predicted 12-week BMI response; key predictors included an unclassified Faecalibacterium species, Oscillibacter sp. 50_27, LDL cholesterol, and systolic blood pressure.
  • The model was externally validated in three independent cohorts: individuals with metabolic syndrome, patients with multiple sclerosis undergoing repeated fasting, and healthy volunteers fasting for 6–12 days.
  • Limitations: total n=38 in the primary trial; predominantly healthy adults; generalizability to clinical populations requires further testing.

Artemisinin and its derivatives for metabolic syndrome: From multi-target mechanisms to translational opportunities (Review)

⭐⭐⭐ · Cao et al. · Mol Med Rep (2026) · Review

  • Narrative review summarizing preclinical evidence for artemisinin and its derivatives in the context of metabolic syndrome (insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, dyslipidemia, hypertension, impaired glucose metabolism).
  • Proposed mechanisms include modulation of AMPK, PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and TLR4 signaling pathways, with downstream effects on insulin sensitivity, lipid metabolism, chronic inflammation, and gut microbiota composition.
  • Evidence base is predominantly cell-based and animal studies; direct clinical data in patients with metabolic syndrome are described as limited.
  • The review identifies single-target therapeutic limitations of current MetS treatments as context for multi-target interest in artemisinin-class drugs.
  • No meta-analytic synthesis, no pooled effect sizes; conclusions are mechanistic and translational rather than clinical.

High-fat diet exacerbates hepatic and adipose insulin signaling impairment and ovarian lesions in a rat model of letrozole-induced polycystic ovary syndrome

⭐⭐⭐ · So et al. · Mol Med Rep (2026)

  • Rat preclinical study using female Sprague-Dawley rats assigned to control (CON), letrozole-induced PCOS (LV), and high-fat diet + letrozole (HLV) groups.
  • Both LV and HLV groups showed increased body weight and fasting glucose, with impaired hepatic and visceral adipose tissue (VAT) INSR/IRS-1/PI3K/AKT signaling vs. CON; no significant difference between LV and HLV for these parameters.
  • HLV selectively increased hepatic PEPCK and G6Pase, and VAT SREBP-1c and ACC beyond LV levels, and produced more severe hepatic steatosis and adipocyte hypertrophy.
  • Ovarian CYP19A1 and AMH dysregulation was more pronounced in HLV than LV.
  • No significant between-group differences for serum lipids, pancreatic β-cell area, INSR, IRS1, IRS2, AKT, PI3K, GLUT4, CPT1A, or ACOX1.
  • Findings are limited to a rodent model; no human data; the combined HFD+letrozole model is presented as a preclinical platform for studying obesity-related PCOS.

Longevity

Gut microbiota dysbiosis shapes brain T-cell immunity in accelerated aging

⭐⭐⭐ · Peschke et al. · Gut Microbes (2026)

  • Preclinical study comparing gut microbiota composition and T-cell profiles in senescence-accelerated SAMP8 mice versus senescence-resistant SAMR1 controls at young adult and aged stages.
  • Accelerated-aging phenotype of SAMP8 mice was confirmed by reduced behavioral performance and brain transcriptomic alterations.
  • Fecal microbiota profiles were obtained using culture-dependent plating and 16S rRNA gene sequencing; T-cell phenotypes were determined by flow cytometry on brain and splenic leukocytes.
  • Strain, age, and sex were identified as drivers of microbiota composition variation; microbial diversity differed between strains independently of age.
  • Several bacterial genera showed altered abundances in young SAMP8 mice; the abstract was truncated and does not provide complete downstream findings on brain T-cell profiles.
  • No human data; findings are confined to a mouse senescence model.

Drug-induced metabolic remodeling promotes antimicrobial activity in macrophages and extends longevity in vivo

  • In vivo longevity extension was reported; the model organism, duration, and quantitative outcomes were not fully available from the provided abstract text.

UA-30 mitigates systemic aging and motor decline by inhibiting RalA signaling in naturally aged mice

⭐⭐⭐ · Zhang et al. · J Exp Med (2026)

  • Preclinical study in naturally aged mice (18.5-month-old) evaluating UA-30, a novel compound, over a 10-week treatment period.
  • UA-30 treatment was associated with reduced frailty, slowed epigenetic age, extended lifespan, improved muscle function, reduced fibrosis, and reduced muscle atrophy in treated mice.
  • Mechanism identified via thermal proteome profiling, surface plasmon resonance, and functional analyses: Ras-related protein Ral-A (RalA) was identified as a direct molecular target of UA-30; UA-30 suppressed RalA activity, inhibiting the pro-fibrotic JNK-Smad signaling axis and enhancing mitochondrial function.
  • Overexpression of constitutively active RalA largely abolished the anti-aging and anti-fibrotic effects of UA-30, supporting target specificity.
  • UA-30 is described as having a favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profile; specific PK parameters and safety data were not detailed in the abstract.
  • No human data; all findings are from a mouse aging model.

Strength & Hypertrophy

Effects of Resistance Training on Muscle Hypertrophy in Children and Adolescents: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Komm et al. · Sports Med (2026) · Meta-analysis

  • Systematic review and meta-analysis of 23 randomized and non-randomized controlled studies; total n = 1,443 participants under 18 years of age; database search conducted April 2025 (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar).
  • Hypertrophy was assessed with direct imaging or indirect instrumented methods; random-effects model used; effect sizes reported as Cohen's d with 95% CIs.
  • vs. passive controls: small overall effect on muscle mass (Cohen's d = 0.22, 95% CI 0.06–0.37); by maturational stage vs. passive controls: prepubertal d = 0.16 (95% CI −0.04 to 0.36), peripubertal d = 0.30 (95% CI 0.01–0.58), postpubertal d = 0.01 (95% CI −0.31 to 0.33).
  • vs. active controls: overall Cohen's d = 0.61 (95% CI 0.03–1.20); subgroup differences by maturational stage were statistically significant (p = 0.0227), with postpubertal participants showing a large effect (Cohen's d = 1.50, 95% CI 0.46–2.54) vs. smaller non-significant effects in prepubertal and peripubertal groups.
  • Sex subgroup vs. active controls: large effect in males (Cohen's d = 1.26, 95% CI 0.29–2.22); trivial in females (Cohen's d = −0.12, 95% CI −1.05 to 0.81); trivial in mixed samples (Cohen's d = 0.04, 95% CI −0.08 to 0.17).
  • Sex subgroup vs. passive controls: trivial in males (d = 0.11), small in females (d = 0.20), small in mixed samples (d = 0.39); none reached statistical significance individually.
  • Inclusion of non-randomized controlled studies and heterogeneity in imaging/measurement methods are noted as limitations.

Lower-limb neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) for adults with kidney failure undergoing dialysis

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Beresford et al. · Cochrane Database Syst Rev (2026) · Meta-analysis

  • Cochrane systematic review of 12 RCTs published 2011–2025; 439 participants randomized across all study arms; 348 randomized to NMES or eligible control groups; 299 contributed to ≥1 meta-analysis; conducted across 8 countries in haemodialysis settings.
  • Intervention durations 4–20 weeks; stimulation frequencies 5–100 Hz; session durations 20–60 min; 2–5 sessions per week; wide parameter heterogeneity noted.
  • Life participation: very uncertain effect (MD 1.00, 95% CI −3.85 to 5.85; 1 study, n=20; very low-certainty evidence).
  • Diastolic BP: little to no difference (MD 2.72, 95% CI −2.85 to 8.29; I² = 0%; 4 studies, n=101; low-certainty evidence).
  • Systolic BP: little to no difference (MD 5.54, 95% CI −4.02 to 15.09; I² = 0%; 4 studies, n=101; low-certainty evidence).
  • Resting heart rate: little to no difference (MD 4.58, 95% CI −2.67 to 11.82; I² = 0%; 2 studies, n=55; low-certainty evidence).
  • Death: very uncertain (RR 0.24, 95% CI 0.01 to 5.26; 1 study, n=22; very low-certainty evidence); fatigue, pain, infection, and vascular access problems not reported in any included study; no peritoneal dialysis populations included.

Non-invasive biofeedback-assisted vs. conventional pelvic floor muscle training combined with high-effort resistance training for urinary incontinence after radical prostatectomy

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Babović et al. · BMC Urol (2026) · RCT

  • 12-week RCT in prostate cancer patients post-radical prostatectomy; n=109 randomized (dPFMT group n=55, pPFMT group n=54); 96 participants (88%) completed the intervention.
  • Both groups underwent a 12-week whole-body high-effort resistance training (HERT) program at >60% 1RM to momentary failure; differing only in PFMT delivery (device-based biofeedback vs. physiotherapist-guided conventional).
  • Primary outcome (24-h pad test): statistically significant urinary leakage reduction in all participants from baseline to week 12 (mean (SD) = −86.39 g (336.25 g); p<0.001); no between-group difference in treatment success (OR = 1.19, 95% CI 0.53–2.66; p = 0.670).
  • Treatment success (≥50% UI reduction) achieved in 52.1% of all participants.
  • Post-hoc exploratory analysis in success-achievers only: greater relative reduction in dPFMT vs. pPFMT (−83.97% (13.88%) vs. −74.68% (12.93%); p = 0.017); no multiple-testing correction applied — flagged for cautious interpretation.
  • Time since surgery was a significant negative predictor of treatment success (p<0.001); age (p=0.313) and follow-up rehabilitation (p=0.994) were not.
  • Study was retrospectively registered (NCT06206993, December 2023).

Supplements

Vitamin D supplementation for prevention of vitamin D deficiency in preterm and low birth weight infants

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Pharande et al. · Cochrane Database Syst Rev (2026) · Meta-analysis

  • Cochrane review of 34 RCTs (5,012 infants); 11 studies (n=1,514) compared vitamin D vs. no supplementation in preterm infants; 25 studies (n=1,546) compared higher- vs. lower-dose vitamin D in preterm infants; 1 study enrolled 2,079 term LBW infants; latest search February 2026.
  • Vitamin D supplementation vs. no supplementation in preterm infants: probably reduces vitamin D deficiency (RR 0.20, 95% CI 0.12–0.35; RD −0.28; NNTB 4; 4 studies, n=385; moderate-certainty evidence); may reduce vitamin D insufficiency or deficiency (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.38–0.58; RD −0.38; NNTB 3; 4 studies, n=385; low-certainty evidence).
  • In term LBW infants (weekly vitamin D3 35 µg from day 7 to 6 months vs. placebo): probably reduces vitamin D deficiency (RR 0.21, 95% CI 0.13–0.34; RD −0.30; NNTB 3; n=453; moderate-certainty evidence) and probably reduces insufficiency or deficiency (RR 0.59, 95% CI 0.50–0.70; RD −0.30; NNTB 3; n=453; moderate-certainty evidence) at 6 months.
  • Higher dose (≥800 IU/day) vs. lower dose (<800 IU/day) in preterm infants: probably reduces deficiency (RR 0.15, 95% CI 0.07–0.31; NNTB 8; 11 studies, n=672; moderate-certainty); may reduce insufficiency or deficiency (RR 0.37, 95% CI 0.28–0.48; NNTB 4; 11 studies, n=683; low-certainty); probably increases vitamin D excess (RR 5.76, 95% CI 1.09–30.3; RD 0.04; NNTH 25; 5 studies, n=395; moderate-certainty evidence).
  • Higher vs. lower dose: little to no difference in radiological osteopenia of prematurity (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.48–1.50; 7 studies, n=447; low-certainty); little to no difference in bone mineral content (SMD 0.13, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.49; 4 studies, n=124; low-certainty); probably reduces secondary hyperparathyroidism (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.31–0.67; I²=31%; NNTB 6; 6 studies, n=409; moderate-certainty).
  • Most studies assessed at high risk of bias; many subgroup analyses underpowered.

Pharmacological therapies for the prevention of fractures in men

⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Braten et al. · Cochrane Database Syst Rev (2026) · Meta-analysis

  • Cochrane review of 17 RCTs (n=4,132 participants); average participant age ranged 52–73 years; included bisphosphonates (7 trials, n=2,548), PTH/PTHrP analogues (4 trials, n=569), denosumab (1 trial, n=240), and romosozumab (1 trial, n=244) vs. placebo; search to October 2025.
  • Bisphosphonates vs. placebo — hip fractures: RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.06–8.51; I²=36%; 4 trials, n=1,635; very low-certainty evidence; 2/875 vs. 2/760 events.
  • Bisphosphonates vs. placebo — symptomatic vertebral fractures: RR 0.49, 95% CI 0.14–1.74; I²=0%; 5 trials, n=1,876; very low-certainty evidence; 5/1,021 vs. 7/855 events.
  • Bisphosphonates vs. placebo — other (non-hip, non-vertebral) fractures: RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.42–1.45; I²=0%; 6 trials, n=2,043; very low-certainty evidence.
  • Bisphosphonates probably do not increase adverse events (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.93–1.19; I²=75%; 7 trials, n=2,548; moderate-certainty) or serious adverse events (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.84–1.08; I²=0%; 6 trials, n=2,457; moderate-certainty).
  • Withdrawals due to AEs very uncertain: RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.39–1.18; I²=37%; 7 trials, n=2,548; very low-certainty evidence.
  • Evidence for PTH/PTHrP analogues, denosumab, and romosozumab on fracture outcomes also very uncertain; no trial reported disability; evidence is largely susceptible to bias in selection of reported results (83% of placebo-controlled trials).

Effects of six weeks of guanidinoacetic acid supplementation with and without creatine monohydrate on cognitive function and markers of health in healthy adults

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Chun et al. · J Int Soc Sports Nutr (2026) · RCT

  • Double-blind, randomized, counterbalanced proof-of-concept exploratory trial; 58 healthy active adults (33 females; mean age 35.5 ± 14 years; 76.2 ± 14 kg) over 6 weeks; three arms: placebo (2 × 6 g/day maltodextrin), GAA (2 × 1 g/day) + placebo (2 × 5 g/day), or GAA (2 × 1 g/day) + CrM (2 × 5 g/day).
  • Fasting blood samples and a battery of cognitive, mood, stress, sleep quality, and health marker assessments collected at baseline and week 6.
  • GAA supplementation tended to improve overall reaction time (−241.8 ms, 95% CI −533 to 50); this result only approached significance.
  • Both GAA alone and GAA + CrM improved delayed verbal episodic recognition memory and retrieval speed, and some measures of perceived stress, sleep quality, and quality of life.
  • Most cognitive test outcomes were not significantly affected; several differences only approached significance; inconsistencies in findings noted by authors.
  • Authors explicitly state additional research is needed before conclusions can be drawn; exploratory/proof-of-concept designation limits inferential scope.

Sleep & Recovery

Effects of a Group-Based Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Sleep in Nursing Students: Pilot Randomized Controlled Trial

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Pérez-Manchón et al. · JMIR Hum Factors (2026) · RCT

  • 2-arm pilot RCT at a private university in Madrid, Spain (October 2022–March 2023); 40 first-year nursing students (85% female; mean age 19.9 ± 1.8 years) randomized equally to CBT-I intervention (n=20) or usual routine control (n=20).
  • Intervention: brief group-based CBT-I using active-constructive learning methodology; control: no intervention; 7-day ambulatory monitoring pre- and post-intervention using Kronowise 3.0 system plus Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI).
  • Largest treatment effect: self-reported sleep latency (β = −19.78 min, 95% CI −33.83 to −5.74; partial η² = 0.194).
  • Sleep duration (β = 36.32 min, 95% CI −5.01 to 77.64; partial η² = 0.086) and sleep efficiency (β = 8.75%, 95% CI −0.97 to 18.47; partial η² = 0.090) favored intervention group but CIs indicated considerable uncertainty.
  • Objective circadian rhythm and sleep outcomes showed generally small treatment effects; 65% of intervention participants rated the program as "very good."
  • Pilot study; n=40 total; single-institution; predominantly female sample; findings are preliminary pending adequately powered confirmatory RCTs.

A Standardized Nursing-Led Protocol Integrated Pain, Sleep, Medication Adherence, and Symptom Management in Postherpetic Neuralgia

⭐⭐⭐⭐ · Feng et al. · J Vis Exp (2026) · RCT

  • Prospective, parallel-group RCT; 128 adults with postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) allocated 1:1 to usual care or usual care plus an 8-week standardized nursing-led protocol integrating structured pain assessment, sleep monitoring, medication-adherence support, and rule-based digital symptom monitoring; 116 participants provided week-8 data (57 usual care; 59 protocol).
  • Primary outcome: between-group difference in change in NRS pain score baseline to week 8; mean NRS decreased from 7.28 ± 1.05 to 3.24 ± 1.28 in the protocol group vs. 7.19 ± 1.08 to 4.82 ± 1.53 in usual care.
  • NRS reduction ≥2 points (treatment success): 76.3% in protocol group vs. 49.1% in usual care.
  • Protocol group also showed larger improvements in PSQI, MMAS-8 medication adherence, symptom burden, and nocturnal awakenings; fewer breakthrough-pain episodes and less rescue-analgesic use.
  • Authors recommend further evaluation in preregistered multicenter trials with intention-to-treat analyses and longer follow-up.
  • No between-group statistical comparison values (95% CIs, p-values for primary endpoint) were reported in the available abstract.

A Novel Synergistic Multilayer Nanofat Technique for Facial Rejuvenation

⭐⭐⭐ · Dragieva & Archimandritis · Aesthetic Plast Surg (2026)

  • Retrospective case series; 42 consecutive patients treated between 2023 and 2025 with a multilayer nanofat protocol combining subcutaneous autologous nanofat injections with intradermal nanofat microneedling (oscillating electronic device) and optional fractional CO₂ laser in a single minimally invasive session.
  • Moderate to marked overall improvement observed in 35/42 patients (83%); skin quality improvement reported in 42/42 patients (100%); improved facial contour (tear trough and midface) in 38/42 patients (90%).

  • No major complications reported; no control group, no blinded assessments, no validated photographic scoring instrument referenced in abstract.
  • Retrospective, uncontrolled, single-center case series; evidence level is limited; authors call for prospective studies.
